02

Project origin

The project began with a conversation, not a software brief

A request for marketing support gradually revealed the opportunity to build a complete platform connecting educational content, client guidance and online booking.

Read the full origin story ↘

The project began with a conversation rather than a software idea. A friend was preparing to open a new wellbeing salon. With extensive professional experience in Lithuania and abroad, he asked:

“Do you know someone who could help me with marketing?”

Instead of recommending an agency, I suggested creating a website with a shareable blog that would allow him to build trust and communicate his expertise directly.

Together we reviewed an existing wellbeing website. Although it contained valuable information and a large knowledge library, the experience felt confusing and text-heavy. We liked the educational approach and the slightly mystical atmosphere, but wanted to create something calmer, more spacious and easier to navigate.

As our discussions continued, I began asking broader product questions. Should clients be able to book online? Would the platform eventually support several therapists? How would languages, content management, dashboards and administration work?

Each new question added another layer to the idea. The shareable blog became a service-discovery experience, then an online booking system, and finally a role-based platform supporting clients, therapists and the practice owner.

03

Discovery and research

Understanding the system before designing the interface

I began by translating emerging ideas into Django models. Mapping the relationships between clients, therapists, services, content and bookings helped clarify the product architecture before interface design began.

Once the underlying system became clearer, I used stakeholder conversations, desk research and realistic client scenarios to define user challenges, personas and interaction flows.

Research was treated as an ongoing part of product development rather than a single preliminary phase. New findings continuously informed the information architecture, interface decisions and backend functionality.

01 Stakeholder interview ↘

Exploring client needs, service delivery and the practical realities of running the wellbeing practice.

To understand whether the initial idea reflected real business needs, I conducted a structured interview with the future practice owner.

Rather than discussing interface preferences, the conversation focused on the people the practice was intended to help, the services being offered and the challenges involved in running a wellbeing practice.

Together we explored questions such as:

  • Who are the typical clients?
  • What motivates them to seek help?
  • What prevents them from booking?
  • How do they choose a therapist?
  • Which information reduces uncertainty before the first appointment?
  • Which administrative tasks consume the most time?

These conversations gradually shifted the project from being a website with online booking into a service platform supporting clients and practitioners throughout their journeys.

02 Product discovery ↘

Using the questionnaire and content to connect client guidance with practice insights.

The questionnaire was designed to create value for both clients and the practice owner.

For clients, it validates their concerns by showing that their challenges are recognised and that relevant support exists. Completing it can build confidence before the first appointment and reduce uncertainty when choosing a service.

For the practice owner, responses provide continuous insight into client interests and emerging needs. They can reveal topics worth promoting, indicate demand for future services or workshops, and inform educational content and marketing decisions.

Educational content was not added after the product had been designed. It was one of the earliest business needs identified during discovery.

The blog helps visitors understand services, answer common questions and build trust before booking. The knowledge library extends that role through a growing collection of resources.

03 User research ↘

Investigating how people seek, compare and build confidence in unfamiliar wellbeing services.

The project began with stakeholder research rather than formal interviews with a large user sample. As the product evolved, I complemented the business perspective with desk research into common help-seeking behaviours and realistic client scenarios.

The research focused on understanding how people approach holistic and mental wellbeing services before their first appointment:

  • What motivates them to seek professional support?
  • What creates hesitation or prevents them from booking?
  • Which information helps them feel safe enough to take the next step?
  • How do they compare therapists and services?
  • Which questions remain unanswered during the decision-making process?

Each emerging insight was translated into user flows, information architecture, interface decisions and backend functionality.

04

Key insights

Research findings became product principles

Four recurring insights influenced both the user experience and the system architecture.

01 Warmth before wellness ↘

One message emerged consistently throughout conversations with the practice owner: the salon should awaken warm feelings before clients even decide to book.

For many visitors, the website would be their first point of contact. Instead of creating urgency or promoting services aggressively, the experience needed to feel calm, welcoming and reassuring.

How it shaped the product

  • Generous whitespace and a slower visual rhythm.
  • Natural colours and soft visual contrast.
  • Educational content before promotional messaging.
  • Compassionate language throughout the interface.
  • A questionnaire that helps visitors feel recognised rather than evaluated.
02 Confidence before commitment ↘

People often seek support while feeling uncertain, vulnerable or overwhelmed. Before booking they want reassurance that someone understands their situation and that appropriate support exists.

How it shaped the product

  • The questionnaire became a primary entry point.
  • Visitors can explore their needs before choosing a service.
  • Service descriptions focus on client situations.
  • The booking journey reveals information gradually.
03 Educational content builds trust ↘

Potential clients often spend considerable time researching wellbeing topics before contacting a professional.

Educational content therefore became part of the service rather than simply supporting marketing.

How it shaped the product

  • A shareable educational blog.
  • A growing knowledge library.
  • Links between articles and services.
  • Content-led discovery alongside booking.
04 Administration should stay lightweight ↘

The practice owner wanted to spend time with clients rather than managing software, so administration had to remain simple and focused.

How it shaped the product

  • Simple booking workflow.
  • Automatic appointment confirmations.
  • Minimal therapist administration.
  • Reusable content management.
  • Focused dashboards with essential information only.

Personas

Designing for clients and the practice owner

Three personas helped balance user needs with business goals throughout product design and implementation.

01 Ieva (34) — Seeking balance through holistic wellbeing ↘

Background

Ieva works in a demanding professional role and generally takes good care of her health. She has tried yoga, meditation and massage before and is interested in approaches that consider both body and mind. Rather than treating individual symptoms, she is looking for a practitioner who can help restore her overall sense of balance.

Goals

  • Find holistic wellbeing services aligned with her values.
  • Learn which practice best suits her current needs.
  • Feel understood before her first appointment.
  • Continue her personal wellbeing journey.

Pain points

  • Many wellness websites focus on selling treatments rather than educating.
  • It is difficult to understand the differences between holistic practices.
  • She wants guidance instead of choosing between unfamiliar services.

Needs

  • Educational articles and practical guidance.
  • A questionnaire that helps identify suitable services.
  • A calm, trustworthy digital experience.
  • Easy online booking.
02 Lukas (49) — Looking for practical relief close to home ↘

Background

Lukas enjoys an active lifestyle and regularly goes jogging around his neighbourhood. After long working days and frequent exercise, he often experiences muscle tension and occasional back or shoulder pain. He is not specifically searching for holistic therapies but appreciates having a trusted wellbeing practice nearby that can help him recover and stay active.

Goals

  • Relieve physical discomfort.
  • Book appointments quickly.
  • Find effective services close to home.
  • Return whenever pain or tension appears.

Pain points

  • Doesn't want to spend time comparing many providers.
  • Unsure which treatment is appropriate.
  • Prefers a simple booking process.

Needs

  • Clear descriptions of available services.
  • Convenient online scheduling.
  • Straightforward information.
  • Confidence that the therapist is experienced.
03 Practice owner — Creating a welcoming space for healing and growth ↘

Background

An experienced holistic therapist with years of practice in Lithuania and abroad, preparing to open a new wellbeing salon. The vision extends beyond providing treatments—the goal is to create a place where people feel welcomed, understood and inspired to care for their wellbeing.

Goals

  • Build trust before the first appointment.
  • Share knowledge through educational content.
  • Reduce administrative workload.
  • Understand clients' interests and evolving needs.
  • Grow the practice sustainably while preserving a personal approach.

Pain points

  • Limited time for marketing and administration.
  • Repetitive client questions before booking.
  • Difficulty understanding which services people are actively looking for.
  • Balancing client care with business development.

Needs

  • A website that conveys warmth and professionalism.
  • Simple appointment management.
  • Client insights gathered through questionnaires.
  • Content management that supports long-term education and marketing.

Together these personas ensured that the platform balanced two complementary perspectives: supporting visitors in choosing the right wellbeing service while giving the practice owner practical tools to deliver and manage those services effectively.

06

Information architecture

Organising the product before designing the screens

Research identified what users needed to achieve. Information architecture translated those findings into a clear product structure balancing educational content, service discovery, booking and practice management.

Read the architecture decisions ↘

Before designing interfaces, I organised the product around the complete user journey and the responsibilities of each role. The information architecture connects the public website, service discovery, authentication and role-based dashboards into one coherent system.

Public experience before authentication

Visitors can learn about the practice, explore educational content, understand available services and complete the self-assessment questionnaire before creating an account. This reduces friction and allows people to build confidence before booking.

Role-based experiences

After authentication, the platform adapts to three user roles. Clients, therapists and administrators each receive focused dashboards containing only the information and actions relevant to their responsibilities, while sharing the same underlying product services.

Designed as one scalable product

The architecture separates user experience from business logic. Shared services such as users, bookings, content, questionnaires, availability and notifications support every role, making the platform easier to maintain and ready for future expansion, including additional therapists, multilingual content and new services.

User flow

Guiding visitors from curiosity to continued wellbeing

The information architecture translates into a simple user journey that gradually builds confidence before asking for commitment.

Rather than directing visitors immediately to booking, the flow allows them to explore educational content, understand available services and gain confidence before making a decision. Each step answers common questions and reduces uncertainty, creating a calmer and more supportive experience.

08

UX decisions

Translating research into product decisions

Every design decision aimed to reduce uncertainty, build trust and simplify the experience for both clients and the practice owner.

01 Build trust before asking for commitment ↘

Visitors often arrive with questions rather than a clear intention to book. Instead of presenting booking as the primary call to action, the platform encourages exploration through educational content, service descriptions and information about the therapist.

This allows visitors to develop confidence naturally before scheduling their first appointment.

02 The questionnaire as guidance, not a form ↘

The questionnaire became part of the user experience rather than an administrative form. It helps visitors understand their needs while providing the practice owner with meaningful insights into client interests and emerging trends.

03 Content as part of the service ↘

Educational articles became a core product feature rather than marketing content. They answer common questions, explain holistic approaches and help visitors make informed decisions before booking.

04 Calm visual hierarchy ↘

Generous whitespace, restrained colours and clear typography slow the reading rhythm and create an atmosphere that reflects the physical practice instead of competing for attention.

05 Progressive decision-making ↘

Visitors first learn about the practice, then explore services, complete the questionnaire if needed and only afterwards book an appointment. Breaking the journey into smaller decisions reduces cognitive load.

06 Designing beyond launch ↘

Although the practice launched with one therapist, the platform was designed to support multiple therapists, multilingual content and future service expansion without structural redesign.

07 Balancing client and business needs ↘

Every feature was evaluated from both perspectives. The product integrates client guidance, educational content, booking and practice management into a single ecosystem where improvements for one user group also create value for the other.

  • Questionnaire supporting both service discovery and business insights.
  • Educational content building trust while improving visibility.
  • Role-based dashboards simplifying administration.

09

Design system

Building consistency through reusable components

As the product expanded from a marketing website into a complete service platform, I created a reusable design system for both the public website and the operational application.

01 Design principles ↘

The system was built around four principles that guided both visual design and implementation.

Warmth

The interface needed to reflect the calming and welcoming atmosphere of the physical practice.

Clarity

Content and actions were prioritised over decorative complexity, helping users remain focused and confident.

Consistency

The public website, booking experience and administrative tools were designed to feel like parts of one coherent product.

Scalability

New pages and features could be assembled from existing tokens, components and layout patterns rather than designed from scratch.

02 Foundations ↘

The design system begins with reusable foundations shared across the application.

  • Colour palette and semantic colour tokens.
  • Typography scale and font roles.
  • Spacing scale and responsive spacing.
  • Border-radius values.
  • Elevation and shadow styles.
  • Interaction and focus states.
  • Day and evening themes.

Primitive colour values are separated from semantic tokens. This allows components to support day and evening themes by changing variable mappings rather than rewriting individual styles.

03 Component library ↘

Reusable interface components were developed for both public and authenticated parts of the platform.

  • Buttons and action links.
  • Chips and status badges.
  • Text inputs, textareas and select controls.
  • Service, therapist and article cards.
  • Responsive navigation.
  • Forms and validation states.
  • Booking controls.
  • Dashboards and administrative components.

Components include hover, focus, selected, disabled and evening-mode variations, helping interaction behaviour remain consistent throughout the platform.

04 From Figma to code ↘

Rather than treating the design system as a static document, I implemented it directly in HTML, CSS and Django templates. Shared tokens, components and layout patterns became part of the production code.

CSS architecture

  • core.css — design tokens, typography, spacing and foundational rules.
  • components.css — reusable interface components shared across the application.
  • pages.css — page-specific layouts and customisations.

This structure keeps global styles independent from page-level implementation and reduces duplicated CSS. New features can be assembled from existing building blocks while preserving the established visual language.

05 Outcome ↘

The design system reduced duplicated styling, accelerated development and created a consistent experience across dozens of templates and multiple user roles.

New pages and features could be built from existing tokens and components instead of being designed independently, making the platform easier to maintain, extend and prepare for future deployment.

10

Django implementation

Turning UX decisions into a functional product

The platform was implemented as a working Django application rather than a static prototype. This allowed the experience to be tested against real data, permissions, scheduling rules and administrative workflows.

01 Architecture driven by product logic ↘

I began by translating the service concept into connected data models. This exposed relationships and edge cases before the interface was fully designed.

Clients, therapists, services, bookings, availability, questionnaire responses, articles and notifications became parts of one system rather than isolated pages.

Main product entities

  • Users and role-based profiles.
  • Therapists and their services.
  • Service categories.
  • Booking requests and confirmed appointments.
  • Therapist availability and unavailable periods.
  • Questionnaire questions, answers and assessments.
  • Educational articles and categories.
  • Client messages and notifications.

Designing these relationships first helped define what each user needed to see and which actions should be available at each stage of the journey.

02 Role-based experience ↘

The application supports three primary roles. Navigation, permissions and dashboard content adapt to each role, reducing unnecessary options and keeping tasks focused.

Clients

  • Register and manage their profile.
  • Complete the self-assessment questionnaire.
  • Book appointments.
  • Review upcoming bookings.
  • Postpone eligible appointments.
  • Receive relevant notifications.

Therapists

  • Manage unavailable periods.
  • Review weekly schedules and bookings.
  • Update professional information.
  • Record received payments.

Administrators

  • Manage therapists and services.
  • Edit questionnaire content.
  • Manage articles and scheduled advice.
  • Review bookings and submitted assessments.
  • Respond to client messages.
  • Manage broader platform settings.
03 Booking and availability logic ↘

The booking system was one of the most complex parts of the implementation. Available time slots are generated from real scheduling data rather than displayed as static options.

The system accounts for

  • Therapist availability.
  • Unavailable periods.
  • Existing appointments.
  • Service duration.
  • Transition time between sessions.
  • Prevention of overlapping bookings.
  • Cancellation and postponement rules.
  • Booking and payment statuses.

Once a client selects a time, the system validates the slot again before saving the booking. This reduces the risk of conflicts caused by outdated availability.

Availability by exception

Instead of requiring therapists to define every working slot, the system treats them as available by default and allows them to block unavailable periods.

Therapists can mark individual hours or complete days as unavailable, while the booking interface calculates the remaining valid slots automatically. This reduces administration and better reflects the needs of a small practice.

04 Editable questionnaire system ↘

The self-assessment questionnaire was implemented as editable content rather than hard-coded interface text.

Administrators can

  • Add and edit questions.
  • Define answer options.
  • Control question ordering.
  • Activate or deactivate content.
  • Review submitted assessments.

Responses are linked to the client and can support service discovery. Aggregated responses also help the practice owner recognise recurring concerns and changing areas of interest.

05 Content management ↘

The educational strategy required the practice owner to manage content without editing code. The administration area therefore supports:

  • Article creation and editing.
  • Content categories.
  • Article images.
  • Publication status.
  • Scheduled advice.
  • Archive management.

This turns the knowledge library into a maintainable business tool rather than a collection of static pages.

06 Multilingual structure ↘

The platform was designed for Lithuanian and English interfaces.

Django internationalisation tools were used for reusable interface text, while content areas were structured according to actual business needs. Multilingual support was applied selectively instead of duplicating every type of database content unnecessarily.

07 Reusable templates and components ↘

Django templates were organised around shared layouts and reusable includes.

Reused patterns include

  • Page headers.
  • Form layouts.
  • Booking slot cards.
  • Weekly schedules.
  • Status controls.
  • Dashboard tables.
  • Pagination.
  • Empty states.

The coded design system was integrated through core.css, app.css and public.css, keeping visual and functional components aligned.

08 Responsive application design ↘

Operational pages were designed to remain usable beyond desktop screens, because clients and practitioners may need to access the platform quickly from a phone.

On smaller screens

  • Multi-column layouts collapse.
  • Dashboard tables become labelled cards.
  • Action groups become full-width controls.
  • Booking slots reorganise into compact grids.
  • Complex weekly schedules remain horizontally scrollable.
  • Administrative forms preserve clear hierarchy and touch-friendly spacing.
09 Implementation challenges ↘

Several UX decisions became more complex once translated into working software. These challenges required repeated testing of complete flows rather than isolated screens.

  • Preserving questionnaire answers across authentication.
  • Preventing guest responses from being attached to the wrong account.
  • Recalculating availability after postponement.
  • Handling buffers around existing appointments.
  • Keeping role-specific navigation understandable.
  • Maintaining consistent day and evening themes across Bootstrap and custom components.
10 Implementation outcome ↘

The Django implementation transformed the original idea of a shareable wellbeing blog into a working service platform.

The final system connects educational content, client guidance, online booking and practice management in one application. Implementing the product against real constraints continuously tested the UX and helped ensure that the designed experience could function as an actual product rather than only as a portfolio concept.

Reflection

What this project taught me

Building A Terapija helped me realise that although my professional roles have changed—from educology and translation to UX and software development—the purpose of my work has remained the same: helping people understand, learn and move forward. I discovered that digital products can become another way of supporting people, combining education, guidance and technology into meaningful experiences.

Read my reflections ↘

Designing the system before the interface

At the beginning of the project I expected to design a marketing website. Through stakeholder conversations and product discovery, it became clear that the real challenge was creating an integrated service connecting education, guidance, booking and practice management.

Delaying interface design until the information architecture and Django data model were established resulted in more coherent decisions and significantly reduced redesign during development.

UX and development are stronger together

Working as both designer and developer meant every decision had to function visually, technically and operationally. Features such as booking logic, therapist availability and the questionnaire became exercises in balancing user needs, business goals and implementation constraints.

Designing products, not pages

The project reinforced the importance of thinking in systems. Consistency across public pages, booking journeys, dashboards and administration became possible only after establishing a shared design system and reusable frontend architecture.

What I would improve

If starting the project again, I would involve end users much earlier through usability testing and structured interviews. I would also introduce analytics from the beginning to understand where visitors hesitate and how the booking journey could be improved.

The biggest takeaway

Good UX is not about creating attractive screens. It is about creating clarity from complexity.

This project confirmed that I enjoy working across the complete product lifecycle—from discovery and information architecture to interface design and implementation. It strengthened my confidence in collaborating with stakeholders, asking better questions and turning early ideas into working digital products.
