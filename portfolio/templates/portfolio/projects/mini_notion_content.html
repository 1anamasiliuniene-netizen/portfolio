02

Research

Evaluating the existing product before redesigning it

The research focused on understanding how effectively the existing application supported everyday project work. Rather than starting with visual redesign, I first evaluated the product structure, user roles and primary workflows to identify opportunities for improvement.

Research objective, approach and methods ↘

Research objective

The objective was to evaluate an existing project management application from a UX perspective and identify improvements without changing its underlying business logic.

Evaluation approach

The product was analysed as a complete system rather than as a collection of individual screens. The evaluation followed the main user journeys to understand how easily users could create, organise and complete their work.

Research methods

Product architecture
Reviewed the product vision, user roles, permissions and overall system structure.
Information architecture
Analysed navigation, project hierarchy and the relationships between major features.
Cognitive walkthrough
Evaluated the primary workflow from the perspective of a first-time user.
Heuristic evaluation
Reviewed the interface against recognised usability principles and interaction patterns.
Workflow analysis
Compared the implemented experience with the expected behaviour of freelancers and small collaborative teams.

03

Research findings

The evaluation revealed patterns, not isolated problems

The heuristic evaluation identified recurring usability issues affecting navigation, task completion and visual clarity. Many findings were connected, showing that the main challenge was not a single interface element but the overall consistency of the product.

01 Navigation and orientation ↘

Moving between pages and workspaces was not always predictable. The interface provided limited support for understanding the current location or returning to a previous level.

  • The active page or section was not always clearly indicated.
  • Navigation patterns changed between different parts of the product.
  • Users could lose context when moving deeper into nested content.

Impact: Users had to remember where they were instead of relying on the interface for orientation.

02 Information architecture ↘

Related information was not always grouped according to how users searched for or worked with it. This made the product structure harder to understand at first glance.

  • Similar actions appeared in different locations.
  • Some labels described system logic rather than user intentions.
  • Important content competed with secondary information.

Impact: Users needed more time to locate information and decide where to begin.

03 Visual hierarchy ↘

The interface contained many visually similar elements, making it difficult to distinguish primary actions, supporting information and editable content.

  • Primary and secondary actions had similar visual weight.
  • Spacing did not consistently communicate grouping.
  • Typography did not always establish a clear reading order.

Impact: Users had to scan more carefully to understand what was important and what could be acted on.

04 Interaction consistency ↘

Similar elements did not always behave in the same way. Differences in controls, layouts and feedback reduced predictability across the interface.

  • Similar actions were represented by different controls.
  • Button placement changed between related screens.
  • System feedback was not equally visible in every workflow.

Impact: Users could not confidently transfer knowledge from one part of the product to another.

05 Task efficiency ↘

Common tasks required more attention and interaction than necessary. Repeated actions and interrupted workflows made routine use feel slower.

  • Frequently used actions required several steps.
  • Users had to repeatedly open or close interface layers.
  • Important controls were not always available at the point of need.

Impact: Routine tasks demanded unnecessary effort and increased the likelihood of losing focus.

Design direction

These findings established the priorities for the redesign. Instead of treating each issue separately, I focused on simplifying navigation, strengthening hierarchy, improving consistency and reducing the effort required to complete common tasks.

04

Design proposals

Translating research into a clearer workspace

The redesign addressed recurring usability patterns rather than isolated interface problems. Each proposal translated research findings into concrete improvements that reduced cognitive load, strengthened consistency and supported more efficient task completion.

01 Simplified navigation ↘

The navigation was redesigned to help users understand where they are, where they can go next and how different workspaces relate to one another.

  • Clear primary navigation hierarchy.
  • Consistent page structure across all workspaces.
  • Improved orientation through active states and contextual navigation.
  • Reduced navigation decisions during common workflows.

Design rationale: Navigation should support users' mental models instead of requiring them to remember the application's structure.

02 Improved information architecture ↘

Content was reorganised around user goals rather than technical implementation, making related information easier to locate and understand.

  • Grouped related functionality together.
  • Simplified labels and terminology.
  • Reduced competing content within each page.
  • Established clearer feature hierarchy.

Design rationale: Users should spend their attention completing work rather than searching for information.

03 Stronger visual hierarchy ↘

Typography, spacing and component hierarchy were refined to create a clearer reading order and highlight primary actions.

  • Clear distinction between primary and secondary actions.
  • Improved spacing and grouping.
  • Consistent typography scale.
  • Better visual emphasis for important content.

Design rationale: Visual hierarchy should reduce scanning effort and guide attention naturally.

04 Consistent interaction patterns ↘

Shared interaction patterns were introduced to make similar actions behave consistently throughout the application.

  • Standardised button hierarchy.
  • Reusable interface components.
  • Predictable placement of actions.
  • Consistent system feedback.

Design rationale: Consistency reduces learning effort and increases user confidence.

05 More efficient task completion ↘

Common workflows were streamlined to minimise unnecessary interactions and support faster completion of routine project management tasks.

  • Reduced interaction steps.
  • Frequently used actions placed closer to content.
  • Simplified workflow transitions.
  • Improved efficiency for everyday tasks.

Design rationale: The interface should support users' work instead of slowing it down.

Design strategy

Together, these proposals established the foundation for the redesign. Rather than improving individual screens independently, I developed a coherent interaction model where navigation, hierarchy and reusable patterns reinforce one another throughout the product.

05

Dashboard redesign

Turning the dashboard into a clearer workspace

The dashboard is the user's first impression after signing in. The redesign focused on transforming it from a collection of information into a workspace that helps users understand their priorities, continue ongoing work and quickly access the most important actions.

01 Reorganising information hierarchy ↘

Content was reorganised around users' immediate goals. Frequently accessed information became more prominent, while secondary content moved into supporting positions.

  • Primary information appears first.
  • Related content is visually grouped.
  • Reduced visual competition between sections.
  • Improved scanning and orientation.
02 Supporting user priorities ↘

The layout encourages users to focus on current work instead of searching for where to begin.

  • Important tasks receive greater emphasis.
  • Clear distinction between primary and secondary actions.
  • Reduced cognitive load during decision making.
  • Logical visual flow through the page.
03 Creating a reusable layout ↘

The dashboard established layout principles that can be reused across future application pages.

  • Consistent spacing system.
  • Reusable card components.
  • Shared typography hierarchy.
  • Predictable placement of actions.

Outcome

The redesign transformed the dashboard from an information display into a workspace supporting orientation, prioritisation and efficient task management. These principles became the foundation for redesigning the remaining application screens.

06

Project management

Organising projects around real working contexts

The dashboard provides an overview, but most work happens inside individual projects. The next redesign stage focused on making project creation, navigation and progress tracking more predictable across personal and collaborative workspaces.

01 Clarifying project structure ↘

The original application supported personal, work and shared projects, but their relationships were not always immediately clear. Navigation reflected the underlying system structure more strongly than the way users approached their work.

  • Personal and work projects were accessed through separate navigation paths.
  • Shared projects appeared as an additional category rather than as part of a connected workspace.
  • Active and completed projects did not follow one consistent organisational pattern.

Design direction: organise projects around their current working state while preserving the distinction between personal, professional and collaborative contexts.

02 Simplifying project creation ↘

Creating a project should help users begin working quickly rather than asking them to understand the complete system beforehand.

  • The primary New project action remains available from the global navigation.
  • Personal and work project types are presented as clear, purposeful choices.
  • Secondary configuration can be introduced after the project has been created.

Design direction: reduce the number of decisions required before users can create a workspace and begin adding meaningful content.

03 Maintaining orientation inside projects ↘

Once users entered a project, the interface needed to preserve context while supporting movement between tasks, collaborators and project-level information.

  • The project title and current location should remain clear.
  • Primary project actions should appear consistently across related screens.
  • Users should be able to return to the project overview without rebuilding their navigation path.

Design direction: establish a reusable project-page structure with stable navigation, predictable actions and a clear visual hierarchy.

04 Making progress visible ↘

Project management is not only about storing information. Users need to understand what is active, what requires attention and what has already been completed.

  • Project status should be visible without opening multiple screens.
  • Overdue and incomplete work should be distinguishable from general project information.
  • Completed projects should remain available without competing with active work.

Design direction: use clear status patterns and progressive disclosure to support awareness without creating a visually heavy interface.

05 Simplifying project organisation ↘

The original application separated projects into Personal and Work categories. During the redesign I questioned whether this distinction actually helped users organise their work.

Instead, the interface was reorganised around project ownership and collaboration:

  • Mine — projects owned and managed by the user.
  • Shared — projects where the user participates through collaboration or assigned tasks.
  • Completed projects remain accessible through a dedicated archive without competing with active work.

Design direction: simplify navigation by reflecting how users naturally think about ownership and collaboration, reducing unnecessary categorisation while making active work easier to locate.

Outcome

The project-management redesign connects project creation, navigation and progress tracking into one coherent workflow. Instead of treating projects as separate containers, the experience supports users as they begin, organise and continue meaningful work.

07

Task redesign

Turning the task editor into a focused workspace

The original page was trying to be an editor instead of a workspace. Every task property appeared inside one long form, making routine work feel administrative and giving configuration the same importance as completing the task.

01 Simplifying task hierarchy ↘

The original page presented every task property as an editable field. Users encountered controls before they could understand the task itself, its context or its current state.

  • The project context is preserved through a clear back link.
  • The task title and description become the primary content.
  • Status, assignee and due date are grouped into a concise overview.
  • Supporting information is displayed as readable content rather than exposed form fields.

Design direction: present information before configuration so users can immediately understand what they are working on.

02 Separating work from configuration ↘

Reviewing progress and completing work are frequent activities, while editing task properties happens only occasionally. The original interface gave these activities equal visual weight.

  • Completing the task becomes the primary visible action.
  • Editing is moved into an expandable Task details panel.
  • Attachments remain accessible without opening the edit form.
  • Deletion is isolated inside a separate danger area.

Design direction: optimise the workspace for completing work while keeping configuration available without allowing it to dominate the page.

03 Supporting collaboration in context ↘

Tasks often become small communication spaces while work is in progress. In the original layout, comments appeared as another administrative form beneath the editing controls.

  • Progress updates remain connected to the task.
  • Users can add links to completed work.
  • Decisions and useful context can be recorded in one place.
  • The discussion area remains visible without competing with the task itself.

Design direction: integrate collaboration into task execution so communication supports the work instead of interrupting it.

04 Extending reusable workspace patterns ↘

The task page was not redesigned as an isolated screen. It extends the visual and interaction patterns introduced across the dashboard and project-management redesigns.

  • Page headers follow a consistent hierarchy.
  • Information and actions are grouped into reusable panels.
  • The primary workspace is supported by a secondary sidebar.
  • Editing and destructive actions use progressive disclosure.
  • Responsive and dark-mode behaviour remains consistent across the application.

Design direction: build task management as another expression of the same workspace system rather than introducing new page-specific patterns.

Outcome

The redesign shifted the task page from an administrative editor into a focused workspace. Users can understand the task, its ownership and current progress before interacting with controls, while collaboration and configuration remain accessible without competing with the work itself.

08

Design system

Turning individual screens into a reusable system

As the redesign expanded across dashboards, projects and tasks, individual interface decisions needed to become a coherent system. The goal was not only to create visual consistency, but to establish reusable foundations that could support future design and development.

01 Establishing shared design foundations ↘

Typography, colour, spacing, borders, radii and shadows were defined through shared design tokens rather than repeated independently across page-specific styles.

  • Consistent typography scale.
  • Shared spacing system.
  • Reusable colour and surface variables.
  • Standardised borders, radii and shadows.

Design rationale: Shared foundations make the interface easier to maintain and allow visual changes to be applied consistently throughout the product.

02 Building reusable interface components ↘

Shared components were created for recurring interface elements so that similar actions and information were presented consistently across the application.

  • Reusable buttons and action groups.
  • Consistent cards and content containers.
  • Shared form and input patterns.
  • Standardised tables, status indicators and empty states.

Design rationale: Reusable components reduce inconsistency and help users recognise familiar patterns across different workspaces.

03 Standardising page structure ↘

Dashboard pages, project workspaces, task views and forms were organised around shared layout principles rather than treated as unrelated screens.

  • Consistent page headers.
  • Predictable placement of primary actions.
  • Shared content widths and spacing.
  • Repeated patterns for supporting information.

Design rationale: A consistent page structure improves orientation and allows users to understand new screens more quickly.

04 Connecting design with implementation ↘

Because the product was implemented in Django, the design system extended beyond visual styling into reusable templates, shared CSS classes and common interaction patterns.

  • Shared Django template structures.
  • Reusable CSS classes.
  • Reduced page-specific styling.
  • Common patterns for future features.

Design rationale: Connecting design decisions with implementation helps the product remain consistent as it grows.

Outcome

Together, these decisions transformed the redesign from a collection of improved screens into a reusable product system. New functionality can now be introduced through shared principles, components and page patterns instead of redesigning the interface from the beginning.

09

Reflection

Designing the system, not only the screens

Redesigning Mini Notion changed the way I think about improving existing products. The project began with individual usability problems but gradually became an exercise in connecting research, interaction design and implementation into one coherent product system.

01 Research before redesign ↘

The research phase helped me move beyond surface-level visual improvements and identify recurring problems in navigation, hierarchy and task completion.

It reinforced that redesign decisions should respond to observed user needs rather than assumptions about what an interface should look like.

02 Designing for scalability ↘

As the redesign expanded, I learned that consistency cannot be maintained by styling each page independently.

Reusable components, shared page patterns and clear design foundations made the application easier to extend without weakening the overall experience.

03 Treating implementation as part of design ↘

Implementing the redesign myself allowed me to test design decisions directly in a working application.

It also showed how interface choices influence template structure, CSS architecture, maintainability and the effort required to introduce future features.

04 Moving from screens to product thinking ↘

The strongest result was not one redesigned dashboard, project page or task workspace, but the relationships created between them.

The project strengthened my understanding that successful digital products emerge from systems in which research, interaction patterns and implementation support one another.

Biggest takeaway

Unlike designing a new product, redesigning an existing one begins with understanding and improving how it works. Only after the functional structure becomes clear does the visual system have a meaningful role to play.
